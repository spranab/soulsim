# The Pulamini-ayana

*The road of one soul across 12 lives, from year 0 to year 470; freed at the last. World of seed 11.*

> Out of thousands, scarcely one strives; and of those who strive, scarcely one knows the road. This is the road.

## Proem

Listen closely to the silence before the first note. This is not a tale of heroes who conquer lands, but of one soul tracing its slow arc across twelve distinct lives, from the dawn of year 0 to the final breath in year 470. We begin in the Satya age, a time of pristine order, where Utnaraj ruled Narikota with heavy hands until his death at 34. The road is long and winding, passing through the Treta age’s shifting sands and into the Dvapara age’s twilight. You will see Amla grow up too fast in Gabikshetra, a child extinguished by year 38, before Kimila wakes as a mystic in Narikota to study the nature of self for over 60 years.

The song holds the weight of Rasumala meditating in Sudragrama, and Mishibhat wandering the dust of Gabikshetra until his body fails at 211. It tracks Rishunish’s quiet vigil in Rimudesha, and the brief, bright flame of Gutra in Kebhapura. You will feel Pulamini’s decades of stillness in Rimudesha, and Suvaraj’s deep breaths in Gabikshetra. Then comes Svasarat, a child again in Sudragrama, before Arshita takes up the path in Kriyatirtha, enduring until year 408. Finally, you will walk with Kachiini in Kebhapura, watching her wisdom deepen through the decades until the very end. There were no dissolutions of the world, only three descents of liberated ones who passed this way. But here, in year 470, as Kachiini’s heart stops, the wheel breaks. The soul leaves forever. This is the promise: not glory, but release.

## Canto 1: Utnaraj of Narikota

The soul entered the flesh of Utnaraj with a heavy hand. He was born in Narikota, among the first breath drawn in the world when year 0 marked the manifest dawn of the Satya age. The boy grew into a man of 30 years, standing at the threshold of a newly opened sky. There was no history yet to burden him, only the raw weight of existence. He ruled over the house, his hands calloused from the work of holding power that had not yet hardened into tradition.

He lived with Tradhita for 33 years. They stayed together until the year 34, when his own breath ceased at age 65. In the Satya age, death was a closing door, not a passage to immediate return. But the soul did not rest. It rose in virtue, yet it carried the dust of its labor into the unmanifest, waiting for another body.

In year 1, when Utnaraj was 31, he bonded with Tradhita. Their years were quiet, measured by the turning of seasons rather than the ticking of clocks. But at 36, in year 5, loss struck him like a stone dropped into still water. He saw the better course, the straight line of duty, and chose the worse. The road bent toward shadow. It was not a sudden fall, but a slow slide, his feet finding purchase on slippery stone he had mistook for solid ground.

Years passed. The world grew louder. At 44, in year 13, success crowned him. He stood atop the hill of achievement, looking down at the valley he had carved out. The impulse rose within him, a hot wind seeking to fan the flames of pride. He saw it rise and mastered it. It was not easy. It cost him sweat and silence. He held his tongue when he wanted to speak. He bowed his head when he wanted to stand tall.

At 46, in year 15, power was given to him. This time, the deed was done in public, before six witnesses. The world watched. They saw good in his restraint. The story of it was told among a few, carried on the tongues of those who had seen the struggle and recognized the victory. It was a small triumph, but it was real. The soul learned that mastery could be seen, and that seeing changed the nature of the act.

At 47, in year 16, loss struck again. It came as a cold wind through an open window. Utnaraj stood still. He saw the impulse rise, the familiar urge to react, to lash out or retreat. He mastered it. His hands remained steady. His eyes remained clear. He did not hide this from himself. He knew then that the enemy was not outside, but within, wearing his face.

The years continued. At 58, in year 27, success crowned him once more. The impulse rose, tempting him with the ease of indulgence. He saw the better course and chose the worse. The slide began again, slow and inevitable. At 60, in year 29, success crowned him a third time. He saw the better course and chose the worse. The soul was tiring, carrying weights that were not meant for it.

Finally, at 65, in year 34, he died. The world called him a ruler. They spoke of his power, his house, his name. But the soul knew the truth. It rose in virtue, yes, but it did not rise high enough to escape the gravity of anger. That was the ruling enemy at the end. It clung to him as he left the body, a heavy cloak he could not shrug off. He returned to the unmanifest, to wait. The cycle would turn again. The soul would seek another vessel, another chance to master what it had failed to hold.

## Canto 2: Kimila of Narikota

The silence between breaths grew heavy. It had been two years since the last small vessel cracked and fell away, a brief stopover for Amla of Gabikshetra, born in year 36, who faced no test and left no mark. The soul did not linger in that void for long. Before that child’s end, there had been Utnaraj of Narikota, ruler, who died at 65 in year 34, his rule curdled by anger. That was the life before the one before this. But now, the weight of what remained unresolved pulled it downward again. The soul came into this body ruled by anger.

It entered Kimila, a woman of the house of Narikota, born in year 42 to Richita and Amveprala. The air of the Satya age of the first cycle was thick with promise, yet her hands were already stained with the grit of impending loss. At 19, in year 61, she bonded with Bishanath. They stayed together seven years, until Bishanath died in year 68. The silence returned then, hollow and sharp. She did not break; instead, at 27, in year 69, struck by loss, she saw the impulse rise. It was a violent, hot thing, demanding retribution or despair. She mastered it. She did this in public, before a single witness. The deed was seen as good, and the story of it was told among a few, a small ripple in a still pond.

Years passed like dust settling on stone. At 34, in year 76, struck by loss again, she saw the better course and chose the worse. The choice was not made with malice, but with the dull weight of fatigue. At 35, in year 77, given power, she saw the impulse rise and mastered it. The authority felt cold in her grip, but she held it steady. At 36, in year 78, lost in uncertainty, she saw the impulse rise and mastered it. There was no fanfare for this victory, only the quiet exhale of a woman who refused to be swept away.

Children came and went. Guka was born in year 87, a small warmth in her long winter. At 50, in year 92, struck by loss, she saw the better course and chose the worse. Again, the dull fatigue claimed the wheel. At 56, in year 98, struck by loss, she saw the better course and chose the worse. The pattern was etched into her bones. Yet, in year 94, at 52, she spoke a teaching the world came to call 'Song of Ember'. What it held has since been lost in the retelling, but for a moment, the ember glowed bright enough to blind the eye.

Kimila died in year 107, at 65, in the Satya age of the first cycle. The world would call her a mystic. Over this life, the soul rose greatly in virtue. The ruling enemy at the end was attachment, not anger. The soul returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body.

## Canto 3: Rasumala of Sudragrama

The wheel turned again, heavy and inevitable, bringing the soul into the flesh of Rasumala. She was born in year 107, in the Satya age of the first cycle, to Kigamala and Yasan in the house of Sudragrama. The air of that birthplace carried the dust of ancient stones and the quiet hum of domestic life. The soul entered this vessel already weighted by attachment, a residue from its previous existence as Kimila, who had died at 65 in year 107. That prior ruling force clung to the new mind like mist to a riverbank.

At 18, in year 125, Rasumala bonded with Bichirun. They stayed together for 29 years, until Rasumala’s own death in year 154. The years between were not empty. In year 130, at age 23, she was struck by loss. The pain arrived like a sudden cold draft in a warm room. She saw the better course but chose the worse, letting the weight of grief drag her downward. It was a failure of spirit, visible and sharp.

Years passed with their own slow rhythm. In year 135, at age 28, she found herself lost in uncertainty. The fog of doubt thickened around her decisions. She saw the impulse rise within her, a hot, demanding thing, but she mastered it. She did not act on it. This was a small victory, quiet and unseen by the world, but real to the soul. She felt the discipline settle in her bones, a stiffening against the tide of desire.

In year 142, at age 35, uncertainty returned. The same impulse rose again, familiar and insistent. Again, she saw it clearly and mastered it. The struggle was less fierce now; she recognized the pattern before it took hold. She had learned to watch the storm from a safe distance.

Then came year 146. Chidumala was born that year, a new thread in the tapestry of her life. Later, in year 151, Bhakan arrived, another voice in the house. But with these births came more loss. At age 39, in year 146, she was struck by loss again. The impulse rose, sharp and bright. She mastered it. At age 40, in year 147, another blow of loss fell upon her. She saw the impulse rise and mastered it once more. Her hands worked steadily; her face remained composed, though within her the war continued.

In year 148, at age 41, she was lost in uncertainty yet again. The old habit of choosing the worse path stirred. She saw the better course clearly, but she chose the worse. It was a moment of weakness, a crack in the armor she had built. She fell back into the mire, not from ignorance, but from fatigue.

Rasumala died in year 154, at age 47, in the Satya age of the first cycle. The world would call her a mystic for the discipline she showed, for the times she saw the impulse and mastered it. Over this life, the soul rose in virtue. It had faced attachment at the end, the ruling enemy that lingered like a shadow. But it had also faced loss with clarity, and uncertainty with strength. The soul returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body, carrying the weight of these choices into the dark.

## Canto 4: Mishibhat of Gabikshetra

The wheel turned once more, and the soul descended into the house of Gabikshetra as Mishibhat in the year 154. Born to Ishdani and Nipuk, she entered a world that smelled of dust and iron. The previous life’s attachment still clung to her like burrs on wool, but now it took the shape of a different hunger. She grew in the Satya age of the first cycle, a time when the light was clear and the air sharp with possibility.

By year 173, she was 19. The sun beat down on the courtyard stones. Suta came into her life, not as a storm, but as a steady current. They bound themselves together, staying side by side for 38 years until the end of that chapter in year 211. It was a long tether, heavy with the weight of shared days and silent mornings.

But the mind is a restless place. In year 171, when she was 17, uncertainty clouded her judgment. She felt the impulse rise within her, a hot coil tightening in the chest. She saw it clearly. She did not let it steer the cart. She mastered it. The air cooled; her hands stopped shaking.

Three years later, in year 174, loss struck her hard. It came without warning, a door slamming shut in the dark. Struck by this blow, she was swept along unseeing. Her feet moved before her mind could catch up. The impulse acted before it was noticed. She stumbled through the days that followed, blind to the path beneath her.

In year 175, at 21, loss struck again. This time, when the impulse rose, she saw it. She mastered it. Later that same year, lost in uncertainty, she saw the impulse rise and mastered it again. The pattern held, though it frayed at the edges.

Years passed like sand through a hourglass. In year 200, she was 46. Loss came to her door once more. She saw the better course clearly, a straight road leading away from the thicket. Yet she chose the worse. It was a small choice, almost invisible, but it left a mark.

By year 208, she was 54. Loss struck her again. The impulse rose, familiar and ugly. She saw it rise and mastered it. Her hands were steady now, or perhaps just tired.

In year 304, at 150 years old, she stood before a crowd that had come to hear her. She spoke of the 'Wheel of Ladder'. She told them that domination does not liberate, but restraint, exercised with care, loosens the wheel’s grip. A soul can leave the wheel forever, if it learns the work.

She died in year 211, at 57, in the Treta age of the first cycle. The world called her a mystic. Over this life, the soul had risen greatly in virtue, shedding the old skins of previous selves. But the ruling enemy at the end was craving. It whispered in her ear as she lay down to rest. The soul returned to the unmanifest, waiting for another body, carrying the quiet weight of what it had done and what it had avoided.

## Canto 5: Rishunish of Rimudesha

The wheel turned once more, grinding down the quiet dust of Gabikshetra to lift a new vessel from the mire. This was the sixth life. The soul, weary but relentless, slipped into the warm flesh of Rishunish, born in year 211 within the house of Rimudesha. Her mother was Chinu, her father Shamit. The age was Treta, the first cycle spinning its heavy, immutable arc. The soul entered this body not by grace, but by necessity; its turn on the wheel had come round again. It carried the old stain, the heavy, sticky residue of the previous death. Before this flesh, the soul had been Mishibhat of Gabikshetra, a mystic who died at 57 in year 211, ruled at the end by craving. That hunger had not vanished; it had merely changed shape, following the spirit like a shadow that refuses to detach from the foot.

Rishunish grew with an ease that felt unnatural, a smoothness that masked the underlying current. The soul never once had to fight its own impulse in this life. It was a terrifying grace. At 18, in year 229, power came to her. She did the right thing without any struggle. The deed was done in public, before 12 witnesses who saw only clarity, not the effortless slide of the spirit. They called it good. The story of it rippled outward, told through a household, then a street, then a town. But Rishunish felt no triumph, only the familiar, cold satisfaction of alignment. At 21, in year 232, power came again. She did the right thing without any struggle. Eleven times over the whole life, the soul acted correctly, not because it conquered itself, but because it had simply forgotten how to fight.

Yet the craving remained, a low hum in the blood. It was not passion for love or ambition for power, but a deep, existential pull toward the sensation of being alive. She bonded with Tidin at 20, in year 231. They stayed together 4 years, until Rishunish's own death in year 235. The bond was real, felt in the skin and breath, but it was also a vessel for that ancient craving. The world watched her fade. She died in year 235, at 24, in the Treta age of the first cycle. The people would call her a mystic, mistaking the absence of struggle for spiritual attainment. They did not see the hook in the eye of desire. Over this life, the soul ended much as it began; the ruling enemy at the end was craving. It had done the right things, but it had wanted to do them with a hunger that could never be sated. With the breath gone, the body cooled, and the soul returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body, carrying the weight of the wheel, ready to fall again.

## Canto 6: Pulamini of Rimudesha

The wheel turned again. The soul shed the flesh of Rishunish, who had died at 24 in year 235, ruled by the same old hunger. Between that end and this beginning, there was only a brief pause in the body of Gutra of Kebhapura, born in year 235, who lived for six years as a child, facing no test, leaving no trace. Now the turn came round once more. The soul entered the body of Pulamini in year 241. She was born to Vemadi and Veranath, of the house of Rimudesha, in the Treta age of the first cycle.

She came into this world already leaning toward craving. It was the weight that pulled her down, the gravity of desire that shaped her early years. But as she grew, something shifted. She did not fight herself. In a life where the impulse and the action were never at war, the path was smooth, unbroken, and silent.

At 16, in year 257, Pulamini bonded with Dhijadeva. They stayed together for 64 years, through the long seasons of their shared days, until her own death in year 321. Their union was not a storm, but a steady current. Around them, the world moved with its usual haste, but within that house, time seemed to settle into a deep, clear water.

When she reached 18, in year 259, power was given to her. She did the right thing without any struggle. The decision came not from effort, but from a natural alignment of will and action. There was no hesitation, no internal debate between what she wanted and what she ought to do. The desire for comfort or gain did not cloud her sight. At 19, in year 260, given power again, she did the right thing without any struggle. This was not a one-time grace but a pattern of being. Over the whole life, 82 times the right thing was done without struggle. Each instance was a small stone laid in a bridge across the chaos of ordinary existence.

The body grew older. The skin tightened, then thinned. The eyes, once bright with the clarity of youth, deepened into the stillness of age. At 80, in year 321, Pulamini died in the Treta age of the first cycle. She had lived a full span, measured out by the number eight decades, but it was not the length that defined her. It was the quality of the steps taken within them.

The world would call her a mystic. They did not understand that no magic was performed, no miracles wrought. There were no visions, no voices from the sky. There was only the relentless, quiet practice of doing what was right when power allowed, without the friction of self against duty. The soul had risen in virtue across this life. It was not a climb up a steep cliff, but an ascent on a gentle slope, steady and sure.

Yet, at the end, the ruling enemy remained craving. It did not vanish; it simply lost its grip as the body failed. The soul returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body. The wheel turned again, silent and indifferent, carrying the weight of the past into the darkness from which it would emerge once more.

## Canto 7: Suvaraj of Gabikshetra

The wheel turned again, bringing the soul into the body of Suvaraj. This birth occurred in year 321, within the Treta age of the first cycle, a time when the world was still young in its memory. He was born to Yanimala and Kedhinath, of the house of Gabikshetra. The soul entered this flesh ruled by craving, inheriting the same weight that had dragged down Pulamini in the previous life. That mystic of Rimudesha had lived to 80, dying in year 321 under the same heavy hand, unable to break the final knot of desire. Suvaraj began his days with that same shadow stretching long behind him.

He grew into the manhood of Gabikshetra. At 18, in year 339, he bonded with Tishashik. Their union was steady and lasting; they remained together for 25 years, a quarter-century of shared breath and routine. This bond held until Suvaraj’s own death in year 364. Within that long stretch, Dabhadat was born in year 357, a child who would carry his name forward. The days moved with the quiet predictability of stone settling into earth.

Then came the moment at 30, in year 351. Power was given to Suvaraj. In that instant, the machinery of his soul shifted slightly. He did the right thing without any struggle. It was not a victory won against himself, for there was no fight within; it was an action as natural as drawing water from a well. This deed was performed in public, before three witnesses who saw the clarity of his intent. The people regarded the act as good, and the story of it spread among a few, carried on the tongue like a small stone skipping across water. It was a single bright point in the long gray line of his existence, proof that the craving had not yet fully calcified his spirit. Over the whole of his life, there would be 36 such moments where the right thing was done without resistance, each one a crack in the dark armor he wore.

Yet the end came as it must. In year 364, at 43, Suvaraj died. He passed into the Dvapara age of the first cycle, the twilight of that era. The world, looking back, would call him a mystic, mistaking the ease of his compliance for spiritual depth. But the soul knew the truth. It had returned to the unmanifest to wait for another body, ending much as it began. The ruling enemy was still craving, patient and unchanged. The wheel did not stop; it merely turned, carrying the weight forward into the dark.

## Canto 8: Arshita of Kriyatirtha

The wheel turned again, grinding stone against stone, until the moment came for the eleventh life of the soul. It did not linger long in the interim darkness. Between the death of Suvaraj and this new birth, the soul had worn the body of Svasarat of Sudragrama for two years only. Born in year 364, that child died before learning the weight of a tool or the heat of the sun, facing no test at all. The slate was wiped clean, leaving only the faint, greasy residue of the old ruling enemy: craving.

Then came Arshita, born in year 366 in the Dvapara age of the first cycle to Mejita and Ishkusik. She was a woman of the house of Kriyatirtha. The soul entered this body still bound by that same hungry ghost, the craving that had ended Suvaraj’s days. Yet Arshita’s flesh was different; it did not fight.

In year 383, when she was 17, the world offered her power. It was a heavy thing, cold and bright in her hands. Most men would have wrestled with the temptation to twist it, to bend it to their own will. Arshita did not struggle. She simply did the right thing. The act was public, performed before three witnesses who stood in the dust and watched. They saw the clarity of her motion, the absence of hesitation. The deed was seen as good, and soon after, the story of it was told among a few. It was a small ripple in a vast ocean, but it rippled true.

That same year, Arshita bonded with Mipidat. They stayed together for 25 years, sharing the quiet rhythm of days until her own death in year 408. Over the course of that life, the soul never once had to fight its own impulse. 32 times the right thing was done without struggle, each time easy as breathing, each time leaving the craving untouched but unacted upon. It was not victory, for there was no battle; it was merely the absence of resistance.

When Arshita died at 42 in the Dvapara age of the first cycle, the world would call her a mystic. They did not know that she had fought nothing. The soul ended this life much as it began: bound by craving, but unable to act on it because the vessel was too smooth, too polished by past grace to catch the hook. The body grew cold in the earth of Kriyatirtha. The story among the few faded into silence. The soul returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body, carrying the same old hunger but no longer compelled to feed it.

## Canto 9: Kachiini of Kebhapura — the Release

This is the twelfth life of the soul. The turn on the wheel had come round once more, bringing it into the body of Kachiini. She was born in year 408, in the Dvapara age of the first cycle, to Likrilak and Rugidran, of the house of Kebhapura. The soul entered this flesh already ruled by craving, carrying the same old weight from the life of Arshita of Kriyatirtha, who had died at 42 in year 408 under that same master’s grip.

Kachiini lived with a clarity that felt like light. At 16, in year 424, she bonded with Muda. They stayed together for 43 years, through the long seasons and the quiet hours of daily work, until Muda died in year 467. The loss was a stone dropped into still water, rippling outward but never breaking the surface entirely. At 59, in year 467, Kachiini bonded with Krikideva. They stayed together for three years, until her own death in year 470.

There were no great battles in this life, no nights of terror where the soul fought its own impulses. It was simply a matter of walking straight when the path offered itself. At 43, in year 451, given power, Kachiini did the right thing without any struggle. The ease of it was remarkable, like breathing after holding one’s breath for decades. The body moved, the will aligned, and the deed was done before hesitation could form a shadow.

At 47, in year 455, given power again, Kachiini did the right thing without any struggle. This time, the act was done in public, before four witnesses who saw the clarity in her eyes and the certainty in her hands. The deed was seen as good by those who watched, and the story of it was told among a few who valued such things. It was not a triumph shouted from rooftops, but a quiet confirmation of what had always been true for this soul: that when the moment demanded, there was no internal war.

Children born to Kachiini included Chidut, born in year 447. The child grew, the years passed, and the light changed from the gold of youth to the silver of age. Over the whole life, the soul had done the right thing without struggle 55 times. Each act was a small stone placed on a path that led nowhere and everywhere at once.

Kachiini died in year 470, at 62, in the Kali age of the first cycle. The world would call her a mystic, misunderstanding the simplicity of what she had been. She had not fought; she had simply not needed to. Over this life, the soul ended much as it began; the ruling enemy at the end was still craving, but it had no power left to hold her. And with this death, the soul left the wheel forever — liberation, after 12 lives. The weight dropped away. The breath stopped. There was no more turning.
