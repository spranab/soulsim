## Prologue: The Founding

In year 0 the world was made manifest, its architecture rising from the void like breath held too long. Nine houses anchored the new reality: Kriyatirtha, Sudragrama, Narikota, Tugakshetra, Pulikshetra, Rimudesha, Latakota, Gabikshetra, and Kebhapura. 200 souls took bodies at the founding, already grown, their wrinkles mapping a history that predated the sky. They moved through these stone hearts with a strange, quiet dignity, for they had known the silence before the noise.

The ages ran in a fixed proportion, a metronome set by unseen hands. First came 200 years of Satya, an era when truth was easy to see and temptation weak. One could look at a neighbor’s hand and know their intent without asking; the air itself seemed to clarify thought. Then 150 years of Treta followed, the light dimming just enough to require effort for understanding. The next century, 100 years of Dvapara, brought further erosion, where truth became a thing to be dug for rather than found. Finally, 50 years of Kali descended. Truth was buried in noise and temptation ruled. The houses remained, but the people within them grew sharp-eyed and suspicious, their whispers carrying more weight than their words.

This age closed in year 500. The chronicler notes not with sorrow but with precision. The manifest world dissolved as it had begun, leaving only the record of its passage. Yet the cycle was not broken; a new Satya dawned, waiting for the next breath to fill the first lungs. The houses stood empty for a moment, then filled again. The count reset. The 200 souls appeared, already grown, ready to walk the same streets with fresh eyes and old memories. The pattern held. It always would.

# Book 1: The First Cycle

## The Satya Age (years 0–199)

The first cycle of the Satya age commenced in year 0, closing only in year 199. It was a period defined by the ease with which truth appeared and the weakness of temptation. At its opening, the living numbered 205 souls; by its end, that count had risen to 1443. During these 200 years, 3660 were born and 2417 died. Yet, for all the life that passed through it, no soul was freed from the wheel.

The age opened with a sense of collective purpose. In year 10, Kadra of Sudragrama was given power. He did well for the world, aided by three others who acted together. But the record notes that even in an age where truth was easy to see, human frailty remained. By year 35, Svavigit of Latakota had been crowned by success. Yet he did ill for the world. This pattern repeated as the years turned. In year 70, Gunimik of Kriyatirtha faced scarcity and did ill for a kingdom; 19 acted together in that failure. A decade later, in year 80, Kihivat of Rimudesha was crowned by success and also did ill for a kingdom, with 18 acting together. In year 93, Puravat of Latakota was given power and did ill for a kingdom, joined by 19 others. These were the deeds most talked of: moments where power did not guarantee virtue, even in the Satya.

Amidst these political failures, works entered the canon. 165 works were recorded in this age. By feeling, they were divided into 59 of fury, 37 of peace, and 31 of wonder. Among them stood the tale-in-verse of Irsrishri. Composed in year 13 and taken into the canon in year 28, it declared: "let the elders say what they will: / I was told to kneel in the Satya; / this drum is my answer / let it burn — some things are only true as ash." Alongside it rose the dance of Inladeva. Composed in year 20 and taken into the canon in year 35, it sang: "the Satya is only weather; you are the season; / I knew your walk before I knew your name; / some threads are older than the loom / meet me where the two paths cross; the stars will not tell."

The lives of the grown who died in this age were categorized by the world as most being mystics or rulers. 1042 were called mystics, and 430 were called rulers. The house of Kebhapura gave the age the most rulers, numbering 99. Among the individuals, Gali of Narikota, a mystic born in year 5 and dead in year 102, was seen given power and did well for a village before seven witnesses; he spoke the teaching 'Path of Seed'. Janadeva of Kriyatirtha, a caretaker born in year 21 and dead in year 115, was seen crowned by success and did ill for a kingdom before four witnesses; he composed one work and spoke the teaching 'Ember of Wheel'. Titalan of Pulikshetra, a mystic born in year 0 and dead in year 67, was seen facing scarcity and did well for a village before five witnesses.

The age closed as it had opened, within the fixed proportion of history. In year 200, the Satya gave way to Treta. The manifest world did not dissolve, but the nature of time shifted, carrying forward the memories of Kadra’s aid, Svavigit’s fall, and the songs that burned like ash or danced like weather.

## The Treta Age (years 200–349)

The Treta age of the first cycle commenced in year 200 and endured for 150 years, closing in year 349. It was an era wherein truth remained largely visible to the eye, yet temptation had begun its quiet pull upon the soul. The population shifted from 1446 living souls at the opening to 1932 at the close; within these borders, 4131 were born and 3642 departed this life. In this time, the wheel of existence was first broken: 111 souls were freed from its cycle. The inaugural liberation occurred in year 250, when Pisuli of Gabikshetra died at the age of 70. The soul that had worn that body left the wheel after four lives, marking a new precedent for the world.

The deeds most spoken of during this span belonged to those crowned by success or struck by hardship. In year 206, Habhak of Tugakshetra, crowned by success, did well for the world. Decades later, in year 248, Kripa of Kebhapura, similarly crowned by success, did well for the world. When the years turned to 296, Viki of Latakota, struck by loss, did well for the world. In year 303, Annideva of Kebhapura, facing scarcity, did well for the world. Yet not all paths were smooth; in year 341, Kusvak of Gabikshetra, facing scarcity, did ill for the world, a deviation noted with gravity.

The cultural record swelled with 98 works entering the canon. By feeling, they were categorized as 28 of fury, 23 of peace, and 14 of valor. Among these, the tale-in-verse of Ishjash was composed in year 95 and taken into the canon in year 201. Its verses whispered: "the wheel turns; I no longer count the turns. / sit with me; the evening is enough / and the evening, being enough, asks nothing more of Ishjash." The hymn of Macha, composed in year 124 and admitted to the canon in year 202, offered a different quietude: "at evening in Gabikshetra the wheel is only a sound; / Macha set down the wanting like a water-jar, / and the path grew quiet / sit. the water-jar is full. it was always full."

Of those grown who died in this age, the world called most of them mystics (1607) and rulers (698). The house of Kebhapura gave the age the most rulers (228), a testament to its political weight. Jisivati of Pulikshetra, a mystic born in year 176 and dead in year 269, was seen struck by loss and did ill for a village before 4 witnesses; yet she composed 2 works. Sidirik of Gabikshetra, a mystic born in year 144 and dead in year 244, was seen given power and did well for a household before 9 witnesses. Vepisat of Rimudesha, a ruler born in year 254 and dead in year 347, was seen given power and did ill for a village before 5 witnesses; he composed 2 works and spoke the teaching 'Bridge of Lotus'.

The age held its shape through these tensions between visibility and desire, between the breaking of the wheel and the persistence of the crowd. It gave way to Dvapara in year 350, leaving behind the echoes of Pisuli’s liberation and the quiet water-jars of Macha.

## The Dvapara Age (years 350–449)

The Dvapara age began in year 350, marking the first cycle’s descent into an era where truth was half-hidden and temptation grew strong. It was a century of ten years less than its predecessor, spanning from year 350 to year 449. The population shifted from 1934 at its opening to 1990 at its close. During these decades, 2954 souls entered the world, while 2898 departed it. Within this turning of the wheel, 342 souls were freed.

The age opened with a significant descent: in year 350, A liberated one came as Kisa of Pulikshetra, who had once been Gadit the mystic. This lineage of liberated beings continued through the century. In year 412, Kisa laid down her body at 88 and withdrew from the world. Later, in year 413, another liberated one descended as Likimala of Kebhapura, formerly Luya the mystic.

The public memory of Dvapara focused on deeds performed under the weight of circumstance. In year 362, Inruhan of Latakota faced scarcity yet did well for a kingdom, with 20 acting together. Four years later, in year 366, Layavat of Kebhapura, also facing scarcity, did well for a kingdom with 20 acting together. Yet not all paths led to light. In year 383, Gidashri of Gabikshetra, crowned by success, did ill for the world. The record notes that Tachita of Gabikshetra, struck by loss in year 403, did well for the world. By the final year, 449, Jidedeva of Gabikshetra, crowned by success, did ill for the world.

The canon expanded during this time. 75 works entered it. By feeling, they were 19 of sorrow, 18 of peace, and 17 of fury. Among them stood the mural of Dibisen, composed in year 248 and taken into the canon in year 352. Its lines spoke of ash on the water and a still lamp, telling slowly what was taken from Dibisen, so that one goes on singing, which is all a mouth can do for a wound. Also entering in year 354 was the dance of Vesu, composed in year 249. It described evening in Gabikshetra where the wheel was only a sound; the wheel turns, and nothing is lost that was true.

Of the grown who died, the world called most mystics, 1127 of them, and opportunists, 678. The house of Kebhapura gave the age the most rulers, 184.

Specific lives traced the arc of the age. Shimet of Sudragrama, a ruler born in year 281 and dead in year 366, was seen given power and did ill for a village before 15 witnesses. He composed four works and spoke the teaching 'Ladder of River'. Duniika of Rimudesha, a mystic born in year 282 and dead in year 376, was seen given power and did well for a village before three witnesses. She spoke the teaching 'Star of Veil' and freed from the wheel at death after four lives. Viladeva of Tugakshetra, a caretaker born in year 310 and dead in year 410, was seen given power and did ill for a village before four witnesses. He composed two works.

The age gave way to Kali in year 450.

## The Kali Age (years 450–499)

The Kali age of the first cycle opened in year 450, marking a period of 50 years during which truth was buried in noise and temptation ruled the hearts of men. When this age began, the living numbered 1992 souls; by its close, that number had risen to 2099. Within these boundaries, 1594 were born and 1486 died. The record notes that 89 souls were freed from the wheel in this age, a count far less than the preceding Dvapara age, reflecting the heavier atmosphere of the times.

The opening decades were defined by ambition and moral failure. In year 455, Srilaga of Kebhapura, given power, did ill for a kingdom, with 19 acting together. The following year saw Matujak of Kriyatirtha, crowned by success, do ill for a kingdom, with 13 acting together. In year 458, Gika of Rimudesha, also crowned by success, did ill for the world. Udtraprash of Gabikshetra followed in year 459, crowned by success, doing ill for a kingdom with 20 acting together. These deeds cast a long shadow over the early years of the age.

Yet, amidst this decline, there were moments of clarity and grace. In year 471, Meja of Gabikshetra, given power, did well for the world. The house of Kebhapura gave the age the most rulers, numbering 112, yet their influence varied widely. Among the grown who died in this age, the world called most of them mystics (491) and opportunists (465). Several mystics left enduring marks on the conscience of the age. Ampraini of Kebhapura, a mystic born in year 367 and dead in year 462, was seen facing scarcity, doing well for a village before 4 witnesses, with 18 standing with them; she composed 2 works. Pejak of Gabikshetra, a mystic born in year 379 and dead in year 476, was seen given power, doing well for a village before 13 witnesses, and spoke the teaching 'Wheel of Mirror'. Ammesh of Tugakshetra, a mystic born in year 377 and dead in year 479, was seen crowned by success, doing well for a village before 5 witnesses; he freed from the wheel at death after 5 lives.

The age drew to a close with a burst of spiritual discourse. In year 480, Likimala of Kebhapura, the descended one, laid the body down at 98 and withdrew. Then, in year 481, a liberated one descended as Vehashula of Rimudesha, who was once Puvisrila the mystic. The final years saw the articulation of four teachings still alive when the record closes. In year 496, Dhasushri of Kebhapura spoke 'Bell of Bell', rejecting the idea that the soul returns in new bodies or that craving breeds anger. Also in year 496, Lagamala of Gabikshetra, a ruler, spoke 'Wheel of River', holding that when dharma falls, a freed one returns. In year 499, Svadeyala of Gabikshetra, a mystic, spoke 'Seed of River', teaching that repeated acts groove the soul and craving breeds anger, and anger breeds delusion. Chimenath of Kebhapura, also in year 499, spoke 'Mirror of Flame', arguing that restraint, exercised, loosens the wheel's grip.

The canon was enriched by 41 works entered in this age; by feeling they were 15 of fury, 10 of sorrow, and 7 of valor. Among them were the song of Panashri, composed in year 335 and taken into the canon in year 450, and the dance of Jidhami, composed in year 149 and taken into the canon in year 452. The record closes in this age, in year 500, leaving behind a legacy of obscured truth but persistent light.
