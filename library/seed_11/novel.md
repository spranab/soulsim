# Arbhani

## 1. The House of Gabikshetra

The air in the chamber of Gabikshetra tasted of dry dust and old smoke, a taste Arbhani had known since her birth in year 220. It was not merely the climate of the Treta age of the first cycle that filled her lungs; it was the atmosphere of expectation, heavy and inert, pressing against her ribs like a physical weight. She sat on the woven mat, her hands resting on her knees, fingers tracing the invisible lines of her palms where fate had already mapped its course.

Her mother, Lapra, moved through the room with a silence that felt louder than shouting. Her father, Chatedat, stood by the doorway, his silhouette cutting the weak afternoon light into sharp, geometric shadows. They did not speak of what remained unresolved. To speak of it would be to acknowledge the debt, and debts in this house were paid in silence or in service, never in words. The soul had come here for a reason, Arbhani knew, driven by the tangled threads of past actions that refused to untie themselves. But she felt no clarity on those threads. She felt only the pull.

From her earliest memory, the ruling enemy had been present. It was not a monster with claws or a storm with thunder. It was attachment. It came in the warmth of Lapra’s hand when she brushed hair from Arbhani’s forehead, in the steady rhythm of Chatedat’s breathing as he slept nearby. It came in the faces of her siblings: Dubha, whose laughter could crack stone; Utsrishi, whose eyes held a depth that demanded to be plumbed; Tevivesh, whose strength was a constant challenge; and Tapaguni, whose silence was a mirror. To love them was to invite the chain. To hold them close was to fasten the link.

Arbhani closed her eyes. The temptation was not to resist, but to indulge. She could let herself sink into the comfort of the house, into the familiar texture of the Gabikshetra name. She could let the attachment wrap around her heart like ivy, soft and suffocating. It would be easier. It would feel like peace. But peace was a trap if it was built on sand.

She opened her eyes and looked at the wall. The plaster was cracked, revealing layers of older mud beneath. Each layer represented a year, a choice, a moment where she could have turned away. She had not turned. Not yet. The weight of the house pressed harder now, testing the integrity of her bones. She felt the pull in her chest, a hollow ache that demanded to be filled with the presence of her kin. It was a hunger, deep and primal.

Arbhani took a breath. The air was still dusty, still heavy. But inside the cage of her ribs, something shifted. A small, hard knot of resolve formed behind her sternum. It did not dissolve the attachment; it merely held it at bay, like a stone in a shoe. She would remain in the house. She would face Lapra and Chatedat, Dubha, Utsrishi, Tevivesh, and Tapaguni. But she would not let them become the world. The Treta age of the first cycle continued its turn, indifferent to her struggle, but Arbhani felt the first true weight of her choice settling onto her shoulders. It was not light. It was not heavy. It was simply there, real and unyielding, as the dust settled on the floor around her feet.

## 2. Youth

The year 245 sat heavy on her shoulders, a weight she had chosen. At 25, Arbhani stood in the center of her own uncertainty, the air thick with the dust of paths not taken. She saw the better course clearly, a straight line of light cutting through the gloom, yet she turned away. The worse path called to her with a siren’s voice, promising ease where there was only depth. She walked it anyway. The choice did not feel like freedom; it felt like sinking into warm mud that slowly hardened around her ankles.

By year 246, at 26, the uncertainty remained but had shifted shape. It was no longer a fog obscuring the future, but a current tugging at her present. She felt the impulse rise within her, a hot stone in her chest demanding action, demanding reaction. She did not suppress it with force, nor did she let it spill over. She mastered it. The act was quiet, almost invisible to an observer, but inside, it was a steep climb up a cliff face of her own making. Her hands trembled afterward, not from fear, but from the effort of holding still when everything in her wanted to move.

Then came year 247. She was 27. The crown of success rested upon her brow, heavy and bright. It should have brought peace. Instead, it brought a moment of clarity so sharp it nearly cut her. Again, she saw the impulse rise. Again, she mastered it. But this time, the mastery did not quiet her; it ignited her.

She sat before the blank parchment, the silence of the room pressing against her eardrums. The inkwell was black as a starless night. Her fingers knew the rhythm before her mind caught up. She did not write for the elders. She wrote for the drum that beat in her blood.

*Let the elders say what they will,* she wrote, the nib scratching like dry leaves on stone. *I was told to kneel in the Treta.* The memory of that command was not pain, but a cold, hard fact, like ice underfoot. She continued, her hand moving with a velocity that blurred the edges of the letters. *This drum is my answer.*

The word *answer* hung in the air, final and unyielding. She could feel the Treta around her, not as a place, but as a scent—cloying, sweet perfume designed to mask the smell of decay. It was a perfume for those who knelt. Arbhani wiped her brow, leaving a smudge of ink on her skin. She looked at the lines she had drawn. They were not a surrender. They were a declaration carved in black and white.

*Arbhani will not kneel,* she wrote, the last letter sharp as a dagger point. *The Treta can keep its perfume.*

She set the pen down. The room was no longer silent; it hummed with the echo of her refusal. The weight of the crown shifted from her head to her hands, where it felt lighter, more manageable than the burden of silence. She stared at the words until they stopped being ink and became truth. The uncertainty of year 245 was gone, burned away by the fire of year 247. What remained was not peace, but a clear, cold space where she could finally breathe.

## 3. The Bond

The dust in the study did not settle; it hovered, suspended in the shafts of afternoon light that pierced the high, narrow windows. Arbhani sat with her hands folded in her lap, feeling the rough weave of her tunic against her palms. She was 45 years old now, in the year 265, and the silence of the house felt heavier than it had at 20. The air smelled of dried herbs and old parchment, a scent that usually brought comfort but now felt like the breath of a tomb.

She remembered Jade with a sudden, sharp clarity. He had been of the house of Kebhapura, a man whose smile had seemed to promise everything and deliver nothing more than temporary warmth. They had bonded in year 240, when she was 20, seeking stability in a world that offered none. For 24 years, they had stayed together, through seasons that turned and years that bled into one another. But Jade had died in year 264, just last year, leaving her with the ghost of his opportunism lingering in every corner of the room. The world would call him an opportunist, she knew, but in her mind, he was simply gone, a hollow space where love should have been.

There was a knock at the door. It was soft, tentative. She rose, her joints stiff from years of sitting too still, and walked to the entrance. Krija stood there, his robes dusted with the same earth that coated the courtyard stones. He was of the house of Pulikshetra, a man whose eyes held depths she could not fathom. The world would call him a mystic, whispering tales of visions and omens, but to Arbhani, he was simply the next step in a journey she had no desire to take alone.

They bonded in year 265. It was not a grand ceremony, nor a declaration of passion. It was a quiet acknowledgment of shared solitude, a merging of two lives that had grown tired of walking their paths separately. Krija’s presence was like a cool stream on a hot day, soothing and strange. She felt the weight of his gaze, not as a burden, but as an anchor in the drifting sea of her grief.

Later, she walked through the rooms where their children had grown. Bachak, born in year 253, was no longer a child but a man with his own life. Irmumala, born in year 258, had married and moved away. Shiki, born in year 263, and Kutraraj, born in year 265, were young, their futures unwritten scrolls. The house felt too large for the few who remained within it. Arbhani touched the wall beside her, feeling the cool stone beneath her fingers. She had lost Jade, but she had gained Krija, and with him, a strange peace settled over her heart.

She looked out the window at the garden, where the trees stood bare against the winter sky. The years would continue to pass, 32 more of them, until her own death in year 297. But for now, in this moment, she was alive, and Krija was beside her. The temptation to retreat into memory was strong, but she resisted it, choosing instead to face the unknown with him. The dust still hovered in the light, indifferent to their lives, but Arbhani felt a strange sense of belonging, as if the house itself had finally accepted her return.

## 4. The Tests

The dust in the chamber of Gabikshetra had a weight to it, fine as ground bone, settling into the creases of Arbhani’s tunic. She sat by the low window, watching the light shift across the floor stones. It was year 258, and she was 38 years old. The air inside felt thick, not with heat, but with the residue of a long day’s labor. Her hands rested on her knees, palms upturned, fingers still trembling slightly from the strain of holding back a desire that had risen in her chest like a tide.

She closed her eyes and let the memory surface. It was not a grand memory, nor one of triumph. It was small, sharp, and entirely private. Year 253. She had been 33. The uncertainty had pressed against her temples, a cold pressure that demanded she reach for something—to grasp, to hold, to define. She saw the impulse rise, clear as a blade in the dark. It promised stability. It promised control. But she had seen the cost of such grasping. She had mastered it. The relief had been hollow, a silence where noise used to be.

Now, at 38, that silence had bloomed into something else. A song. It had begun in her throat before her mind could judge it, born from the memory of a moment four years prior, when she was 34 and crowned by success. In that earlier hour, she had seen the impulse again, seductive and bright, and mastered it with a stillness that surprised even her. That stillness had not disappeared; it had settled into her bones, becoming the rhythm of her breathing, the beat of her heart.

She opened her eyes and looked at the empty space before her. The words came without effort, pulling themselves from the quiet center of her being. They were not meant for others. They were a ledger of her own soul, written in air.

"Arbhani set down the wanting like a water-jar," she whispered, the syllables rough but true. "And the path grew quiet."

She paused, listening to the house settle around her. The wheels of Gabikshetra turned with a steady, unyielding grace. She felt the truth of it in her ribs. The wheel turns; Gabikshetra turns with it. Nothing is lost that was true.

The temptation had not vanished entirely. It lingered at the edge of her vision, a shadow waiting for her to look away from the present moment and reach for the past or future. But she held her gaze fixed on the dust motes dancing in the slant of sunlight. She felt the weight of the choice she had made, not just once, but repeatedly, across the span of years. It was a burden, yes, but it was also a structure. Without it, the house would have collapsed into chaos. With it, there was order. There was peace.

She stood up, her joints protesting slightly, and walked to the hearth. The fire had burned down to embers, glowing with a soft, persistent heat. She added a small stick, watching it catch. The act was mundane, necessary. It mirrored the internal work she had done. To keep the fire alive required attention, not force. To keep the self intact required mastery, not suppression.

She returned to her seat and sat in the quiet. The song remained in her mouth, a taste of ash and honey. She knew that tomorrow would bring new uncertainties, new impulses, new chances to choose the worse course or the better one. But for now, in this room, with the light fading from gold to grey, there was only the truth she had spoken. It was enough. It was all she had. And it was true.

## 5. The Turning

The air in the house of Gabikshetra had grown thin, brittle as old parchment. It was year 284, and Arbhani stood at 64, a number that felt less like an age and more like a weight pressed into her spine. The last 15 years had not softened her; they had carved her. She moved through the corridors with the careful hesitation of one who knows the floorboards groan under specific steps, avoiding the silence where laughter used to reside.

Jade had been gone for 20 years. In year 264, when Arbhani was 44, the grief had been a physical blow, a sudden vacuum in the chest that left her gasping for breath she could not draw. They had spent 24 years together, and now that span was nothing but dust on the window sills. But Jade’s absence was a known quantity, a familiar wound. This new loss was different. It was fresh, bleeding.

Kutraraj. The name tasted like iron in her mouth. He had died in year 280, at 15. Only four years ago, yet it felt like a lifetime ago, a lifetime too long. Arbhani remembered the small hands, now cold, and the way he had looked at her before the end, not with fear, but with a quiet understanding that broke her more than any screaming could have. She outlived him. The words sat heavy in her stomach, undigested.

The impulse rose then, sharp and insistent. It was not anger, precisely, nor sorrow. It was a pull, a gravitational tug toward the edge of the world, toward the quiet dark where Kutraraj awaited. To see him again. To rest. The temptation was sweet, like ripened fruit hanging low on a branch. It whispered that it would be easy to let go, to step off the path and into the void.

Arbhani closed her eyes. She could feel the heat of the sun through the lattice windows, the dust motes dancing in the light. She could hear the distant sound of water being drawn from the well. Life, stubborn and relentless, continued around her. But inside, she was cold.

She saw the better course. It lay in turning back, in breathing deeply, in accepting the pain as the price of existence. It was the path of endurance, steep and rocky, but it led forward. Instead, Arbhani chose the worse. She let the darkness take her hand. It was not a dramatic plunge, but a slow surrender, a yielding to the weight that had been building for 15 years. The choice settled over her like a shroud, heavy and final. There was no relief in it, only the hollow echo of an empty room. She stood there, 64 years old, in the house of Gabikshetra, and let the silence win.

## 6. The Last Year

The air in the chamber hung heavy with the scent of dried jasmine and old dust, a cloying sweetness that clung to the back of Arbhani’s throat. She sat upon the woven mat, her spine straight but rigid, feeling the years press against her ribs like stones. Year 297 had arrived with a silence so profound it felt less like peace and more like the holding of breath before a fall. At 77, her body was a map of worn paths; every joint clicked a quiet protest against the shifting light.

Outside, the world waited in the Treta age of the first cycle, oblivious to the precise moment a soul prepared to untether itself from the flesh it had inhabited for nearly eight decades. Arbhani knew this. The knowledge was not a comfort but a weight, a heavy stone in her stomach that made her hungry and hollow at the same time.

She closed her eyes, searching for the stillness she had cultivated since birth in year 220. But the mind, once a clear lake, was now troubled water. The rule of her life was simple: the soul returns to the unmanifest, to wait for another body. Yet, as the darkness behind her eyelids deepened, the final test presented itself not as a roar, but as a whisper. Attachment. It did not come as a desire for wealth or power, but as a desperate, clawing need to remain, to hold onto the warmth of the sun on her skin, the memory of the house of Gabikshetra, the familiar ache of her own identity.

Her hand twitched toward the oil lamp, a reflex to preserve light, to preserve self. The temptation was visceral, a physical pull in her chest that threatened to snap her focus. She could feel the ghost of Chibadeva already waiting, the turn on the wheel coming round with inevitable gravity. To let go meant to cease being Arbhani. It meant surrendering the narrative she had spent 77 years weaving.

She exhaled, a long, shuddering breath that seemed to drain her strength. The choice was not made with heroism, but with exhaustion. She watched the flame flicker and stabilize, then deliberately turned her gaze away from it. The light did not vanish; it merely ceased to matter. In that moment of letting go, the weight lifted, not because it was gone, but because she had stopped carrying it. The room remained empty of gods or signs, filled only with the dust motes dancing in the dying afternoon sun, indifferent to her ascent. She waited, not for release, but for the next step, which required no more than this single, quiet refusal to cling.

## 7. What the World Kept

The dust in the hall of Gabikshetra tasted of dry earth and old stone, coating Arbhani’s tongue with a familiar grit. She stood by the high window, watching the light shift across the floorboards, marking the slow passage of hours rather than years. The air was still, heavy with the silence that follows a long speech or a long grief. She thought of the weight of names, how they settle on the shoulders like wet wool.

Bachak had died in year 320 at 67; the world called him an opportunist. She remembered his hands, always moving, always grasping, never quite holding anything for long. There was a hunger in him that mirrored her own, though hers had been turned inward, pruned and shaped. He had taken what came; she had waited for what was right. The distinction felt sharp, like a knife edge against the skin.

Nearby, Irmumala had ruled until year 347 at 89; the world called her a ruler. Arbhani recalled the way Irmumala sat, spine straight as a spear shaft, eyes unblinking. Power was a cold stone held in the palm. It did not warm the hand, but it defined the shape of the grip. Shiki had shared that title, ruling until year 333 at 70; the world called him a ruler. His rule had been softer, perhaps, but no less firm in its boundaries. Men built walls of law and habit; women built them of will and patience.

And then there was Kutraraj, who died in year 280 at 15; the world called him a ruler. The absurdity of it tasted bitter on Arbhani’s tongue. A boy king, a crown too heavy for thin bones. He had ruled while still growing, his voice cracking into authority that did not yet fit him. It made her wonder if power was ever truly owned, or merely borrowed from the future.

She turned from the window. The room felt smaller now, the walls closing in with the quiet pressure of memory. She touched the hem of her robe, feeling the weave of it against her fingers. Twelve times the impulse had been seen and mastered over her life; six times the better was seen and the worse chosen. The math of her soul was not written on stone but etched in the marrow. It was a ledger of breaths, of hesitations, of steps taken and retraced.

The light faded from the floorboards. Night was coming, not as an end, but as a return. She waited for it, not with fear, but with the readiness of one who knows the shape of the dark. The soul rose, slowly, shedding the weight of the day like dust from a cloak.
