# The Pulamini-ayana

*The road of one soul across 12 lives, from year 0 to year 470; freed at the last. World of seed 11.*

> Out of thousands, scarcely one strives; and of those who strive, scarcely one knows the road. This is the road.

## Proem

Hear me now, before the final breath leaves Kachiini’s lips in year 470. I sing not of gods who watch from distant skies, but of one soul’s weary tread across twelve lives, a path worn deep into the earth between year 0 and the end. You will feel the weight of Utnaraj’s crown in Narikota, heavy with power yet hollow, as he rules until age 34. Then comes the brief, bright flicker of Amla in Gabikshetra, a child who sees only the first days of change before vanishing at 38.

You will walk the misty paths with Kimila in Narikota, learning that silence speaks louder than swords, a lesson held for 65 years until year 107. From there, Rasumala in Sudragrama carries that same quiet fire, teaching mystics until his body fails at 154. Mishibhat continues the work in Gabikshetra, weathering decades until age 211, when Rishunish takes up the mantle in Rimudesha for only 24 years. The wheel turns again to Gutra, a child in Kebhapura who rests briefly before Pulamini rises in Rimudesha, mastering the art of seeing through the veil for 80 years.

Suvaraj follows in Gabikshetra, enduring until year 364, then Svasarat flashes briefly in Sudragrama as a child. The soul takes its next form in Kriyatirtha and remains there until year 408. Finally, Kachiini in Kebhapura bears the last burden. Across these years, through Satya, Treta, and Dvapara ages, the world saw no dissolution, only three descents of those already free. This song ends where the soul leaves the wheel forever with the death of Kachiini.

## Canto 1: Utnaraj of Narikota

The soul entered the flesh of Utnaraj, a man from the house of Narikota, into a body already ruled by craving. He was 30 years old when the world became manifest in year 0, during the Satya age of the first cycle. The air was thick with the dust of beginnings, and the hands that built were rough with the labor of making things real. Utnaraj moved through these early days with a heaviness that had nothing to do with fatigue and everything to do with desire. He was among the first, standing on ground that had not yet settled, feeling the tremor of a new existence in his bones.

In year 1, when he was 31, he bonded with Tradhita. Their days were measured not in years but in the quiet accumulation of shared mornings and the dark intimacy of nights spent together. They stayed for 33 years, a long time in a world that had only just begun to count them. But in year 5, when Utnaraj was 36, loss struck him like a physical blow. The grief was a hollow space behind his ribs. In that hollow, he saw the better course clearly, yet he chose the worse. It was not a mistake of ignorance but a failure of will. The impulse rose, hot and sharp, and he let it take the wheel.

Years passed. The world grew accustomed to him. He was called a ruler, though the title felt like a coat borrowed from another man. In year 13, at 44, success crowned him. He stood high, looking down on the fields he had helped sow. He saw the impulse rise within him, the urge to take more, to stretch further. But this time, he mastered it. The restraint was cold and precise. It cost him something, a small piece of his warmth, but he paid it.

In year 15, at 46, he was given power. He exercised it in public, and the deed was seen as good. The deed was seen as good, and the story was told among a few. It spread like smoke, thin and rising. Yet the soul knew the truth of it. In year 16, at 47, struck by loss again, he saw the impulse rise and mastered it. The pattern held.

But the pattern broke in year 27, when he was 58. Crowned by success again, he saw the better course and chose the worse. The choice was not sudden; it was the result of years of small surrenders. In year 29, at 60, crowned once more by success, he did the same. He saw the path of virtue and turned toward the shadow.

He died in year 34, at 65, still in the Satya age of the first cycle. The world would call him a ruler. It burned hot, consuming the last of his breath. The soul returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body, carrying the weight of what it had seen and what it had chosen.

## Canto 2: Kimila of Narikota

This is the third life of the soul. Between the first and this one, the soul had worn the body of Amla of Gabikshetra for 2 years only, born in year 36, and died a child, having faced no test. Before that brief interlude, the soul had been Utnaraj of Narikota, a ruler, who died at 65 in year 34, ruled at the end by anger. Now it came into the body of Kimila, born in year 42, in the Satya age of the first cycle, to Richita and Amveprala. Why this birth? Much was left unresolved. The soul entered this life carrying the influence of anger.

At 19, in year 61, Kimila bonded with Bishanath. They stayed together 7 years, until Bishanath died in year 68. The loss was a hollow space in the room, in the breath, in the morning light. At 27, in year 69, struck by that loss, Kimila saw the impulse rise and mastered it. This was done in public, before a single witness; the deed was seen as good, and the story of it was told among a few. Then at 27, in year 69, Kimila bonded with Utsha. They stayed together 38 years, until Kimila's own death in year 107.

Children born to Kimila: Guka (born year 87). The child’s weight in the arms, the smell of milk and dust, the quiet hours of feeding and watching the dust motes dance in the sunbeam. At 34, in year 76, struck by loss, Kimila saw the better course and chose the worse. The choice was not loud; it was a slow settling into comfort, a turning away from the hard path because the easier one was paved with familiar stones. At 35, in year 77, given power, Kimila saw the impulse rise and mastered it. Power is a heavy cloak, warm but suffocating, lined with the expectations of others.

At 36, in year 78, lost in uncertainty, Kimila saw the impulse rise and mastered it. Uncertainty is the fog that swallows the road, making every step a guess. At 50, in year 92, struck by loss, Kimila saw the better course and chose the worse. Again, the familiar comfort of avoidance, the soft lie rather than the hard truth. At 56, in year 98, struck by loss, Kimila saw the better course and chose the worse. The pattern was etched deep, a groove worn into the mind by repeated walking.

In year 94, at 52, Kimila spoke a teaching the world came to call 'Song of Ember'. What it held has since been lost in the retelling. It was not wisdom from on high, but the raw heat of a life lived in the fire. The world would call her a mystic, but she was only a woman who had burned through much. Kimila died in year 107, at 65, in the Satya age of the first cycle. Over this life the soul rose greatly in virtue. The ruling enemy at the end was attachment. Not anger, not fear, but the desperate grip on what is held dear, the refusal to let go even as the hand weakens. The soul returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body. The silence was not empty; it was full of the echoes of love and loss, of choices made and missed, of the long, slow work of becoming less bound by the world that had been so vividly lived.

## Canto 3: Rasumala of Sudragrama

The wheel turned once more, and the soul, still heavy with the residue of its last existence, entered the house of Sudragrama. It did so ruled by that old companion, attachment, the same shadow that had followed Kimila of Narikota into her grave at 65 in year 107. The world called this era the Satya age of the first cycle, a time of light, but the soul knew it was merely another room in an endless house. It took flesh as Rasumala, born to Kigamala and Yasan in that same year 107, carrying forward the weight of what it had failed to release.

For many years, the body lived at ease, a deceptive comfort that softened the edges of resolve. But life demanded its due. In year 125, when Rasumala was 18, she bonded with Bichirun. They stayed together for 29 years, a long stretch of shared days that anchored the soul in the mundane. Within this union came new ties: Chidumala, born in year 146, and Bhakan, born in year 151. These children became new centers of gravity, pulling the soul deeper into the web of affection it had sworn to transcend.

Yet the mind was not entirely dormant. In year 130, at 23, Rasumala was struck by a loss sharp enough to wake her conscience. She saw the better course clearly, the path of detachment and duty, but chose the worse. She retreated into comfort, choosing the familiar pain over the unknown work. The impulse had acted unseen ten times over the course of her life.

By year 135, at 28, uncertainty clouded her vision again. Lost in the fog of daily existence, she felt the old impulse rise within her. This time, she saw it. She recognized its shape and mastered it, holding it back with a deliberate act of will. It was a small victory, but it proved the soul was still learning to watch itself. The same pattern repeated in year 142, at 35. Lost in uncertainty once more, she faced the rising impulse and mastered it again. Mastery was not yet instinct; it remained a labor, a constant vigilance against the drift of habit.

At thirty-nine in year 146, struck by loss, she saw the impulse rise and mastered it. She saw the impulse rise and mastered it. A year later, in year 147, another blow fell, another instance of loss that tested her resolve. She saw the impulse rise and mastered it. The work was slow, unglamorous, and relentless.

But in year 148, at 41, the old weakness returned. Lost in uncertainty, she saw the better course and chose the worse. Over the whole life, there were times when the right thing was done without struggle, 21 such moments where the soul moved with natural grace. Yet the balance remained precarious.

Rasumala died in year 154, at 47. The world would call her a mystic, praising her depth and her piety, unaware of the internal wars that had defined her days. Over this life the soul rose in virtue. But attachment remained the ruling enemy at the end, not yet defeated, only managed. The soul returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body, carrying the scars of this struggle into the silence.

## Canto 4: Mishibhat of Gabikshetra

The wheel turned again. This was the fifth life of the soul, and before this body, it had been Rasumala of Sudragrama, a mystic who died at 47 in year 154, ruled at the end by attachment. Now, the turn came round once more. In year 154, in the Satya age of the first cycle, Mishibhat was born, a woman of the house of Gabikshetra, to Ishdani and Nipuk. The soul entered this flesh already ruled by attachment, carrying the heavy, familiar weight of that old enemy.

The years unspooled with the quiet inevitability of stone falling into water. At 17, in year 171, Mishibhat found herself lost in uncertainty. She saw the impulse rise within her, a dark current pulling at the edges of her will. She saw the impulse rise and mastered it. The mastery did not come easily, but it came.

At 20, in year 174, struck by loss, she was swept along unseeing. Struck by loss, Mishibhat was swept along unseeing. The impulse acted before it was noticed. She did not see it until the damage was done, a regret settling into the bones of her hands. But the soul was learning. At 21, in year 175, struck by loss again, she saw the impulse rise and mastered it. Lost in uncertainty, she saw the impulse rise and mastered it.

The body grew older, but the work remained. At 19, Mishibhat had bonded with Suta. They stayed together until her death. Their life was not free of shadow, but it was shared. In year 200, at 46, struck by loss, she saw the better course and chose the worse. It was a rare failure, a slip on dry ground. She felt the weight of that choice, how it pulled her down even as she stood upright. Yet, in year 208, at 54, struck by loss once more, she saw the impulse rise and mastered it. The pattern held.

The world would later call her a mystic, but in the moment, it was just work. It was the daily labor of keeping the eye open. In year 304, far beyond the end of this life’s span, at 150, she spoke a teaching the world came to call 'Wheel of Ladder'. She held that it is not so that domination liberates; restraint, exercised, loosens the wheel's grip; a soul can leave the wheel forever. These words were not new inventions but the sum of what had been seen and done in the quiet spaces between breaths.

Mishibhat died in year 211, at 57, in the Treta age of the first cycle. Over this life, the soul rose greatly in virtue. The ruling enemy at the end was craving, a final, faint whisper against the silence. The soul returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body, carrying the residue of these struggles like dust on a cloak.

## Canto 5: Rishunish of Rimudesha

The wheel turned again, heavy and inevitable, carrying the soul into the sixth life. The soul came into this body ruled by craving. The soul entered the house of Rimudesha, where Rishunish was born in year 211. She was a woman of flesh and bone, daughter to Chinu and Shamit, born in the Treta age of the first cycle. The soul did not resist this descent. It accepted the weight of it, knowing that craving still held the reins.

Rishunish grew. Over the whole life, the soul never once had to fight its own impulse. This was the curse and the gift of this life: she never once had to fight herself. The right thing came easily, naturally, as if the world were aligned for her convenience. At 18, in year 229, power was placed in her hands. She did the right thing without any struggle. It was done in public, before 12 witnesses who saw the deed and recognized its goodness. The story of it spread through a household, carried on tongues that praised her ease.

She stayed with Tidin, bonding at 20, in year 231. They were together for 4 years, until Rishunish’s own death in year 235. It was a short union, but it felt complete to those who lived it. At 21, in year 232, given power again, she did the right thing without any struggle. The ease was absolute. There was no hesitation, no shadow of doubt, only the smooth execution of duty. Over the whole life, 11 times the right thing was done without struggle. Each time, it required no effort, no sacrifice, no overcoming of self. It was simply what she did.

But the soul knew the truth beneath the ease. The ruling enemy at the end was craving. It had not been conquered; it had been indulged. The lack of struggle was not virtue; it was a different kind of attachment. The soul returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body. The world would call Rishunish a mystic, seeing only the external grace, the absence of friction. They did not see the internal stagnation, the way the soul remained bound to its desires even as they were satisfied.

At 24, in year 235, in the Treta age of the first cycle, Rishunish died. The body ceased. The soul detached from the flesh, returning to the unmanifest. It did not rest. It waited there, in the void between lives, for another body to be assigned. The craving remained, untouched and unexamined, waiting to drive the next turn of the wheel. The soul carried the weight of this life, not as a lesson learned, but as a debt unpaid. It moved on, silent and heavy, toward the next birth.

## Canto 6: Pulamini of Rimudesha

The wheel turned again, grinding the dust of ages into the same fine powder. It is not that the soul chose this path; it was merely its turn on the great, indifferent mechanism. The body arrived in year 241, born to Vemadi and Veranath, named Pulamini, a daughter of the house of Rimudesha. She emerged into the Treta age of the first cycle, a time when the light seemed sharper, the air clearer, though the weight of existence remained unchanged.

Before this incarnation, the soul had worn the body of Rishunish, who died at 24 in year 235, ruled at the end by craving. Then there was the brief, silent interlude of Gutra of Kebhapura, a life of only six years, born in year 235 and dead as a child, having faced no test at all. Those lives were shadows, quick fades in the long exposure. But this body, Pulamini’s, had substance.

She came into this world already bound by craving. It was not a sudden storm but the baseline weather of her being. Yet, when she reached 18, in year 259, power was given to her. She did the right thing without any struggle. The resistance that usually gnaws at the edges of will, the friction between desire and duty, was absent. At 19, in year 260, power came again, and once more she acted rightly, effortlessly.

This lack of internal war defined her years. Over the whole life, 82 times the right thing was done without struggle. It was not that she never felt temptation; it was that when action was required, the path lay open before her feet, clear as water in a glass. There was no climbing, only walking.

At 16, in year 257, she bonded with Dhijadeva. They stayed together 64 years, their lives intertwined like roots under the same stone, until Pulamini’s own death in year 321. In year 286, Shinani was born to them, a child of that long, steady union.

Pulamini died in year 321, at 80, in the Treta age of the first cycle. The world would call her a mystic, not because she sought the divine in silence, but because her will had become so aligned with the good that effort vanished from it. Over this life, the soul rose in virtue, climbing a slope without fatigue.

Yet, at the threshold, the old enemy waited. The ruling enemy at the end was craving. It did not break her; it simply lingered, the final dust on the scales. The soul, having risen, having acted with ease for decades, finally let go. It returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body, carrying the quiet memory of a life where the heart and the hand moved as one, even while craving held the reins until the very last breath.

## Canto 7: Suvaraj of Gabikshetra

The wheel turned again, and the soul found itself in the flesh of Suvaraj. He was born in year 321, into the house of Gabikshetra, under the cold eye of the Treta age of the first cycle. His father was Kedhinath, his mother Yanimala. The soul arrived ruled by craving, the same enemy that had closed the eyes of Pulamini. It did not know it yet, but this body would be long, and the struggle would be subtle.

The years passed like water over smooth stone. At 18, in year 339, Suvaraj bonded with Tishashik. Their union was a quiet shelter, lasting 25 years until the end of his own days in year 364. Children came into their lives; Dabhadat was born in year 357, a small heartbeat in the long rhythm of the house. But the soul’s attention was not on the cradle. It was on the silence between thoughts, where craving lurked like smoke beneath a door.

At 30, in year 351, power came to Suvaraj. The moment arrived, sharp and clear. He did the right thing. There was no hesitation, no internal war, no grinding of teeth or bargaining with conscience. The action flowed from him as naturally as breath. It was done in public, before three witnesses who saw only a good deed, not the void behind it. The story of it traveled among a few people, whispered in markets and halls, praised for its virtue. But to the soul, it was nothing more than the absence of resistance. This was not mastery; it was still the old habit, smooth and effortless, masking the root.

Across the whole of this life, there were 36 such moments where the right thing was done without struggle. Each one felt like grace, like liberation, like the end of the path. The soul mistook ease for enlightenment. It did not see that craving had not been defeated; it had merely been pacified by convenience. The ruling enemy remained, sleeping in the chest, waiting for the moment when power would fade and desire would return untempered.

Suvaraj died in year 364, at 43, in the Dvapara age of the first cycle. The world, looking from the outside, called him a mystic. They saw the calm face, the good deeds, the lack of visible turmoil. They did not see how often he had chosen right without fighting, nor the single thread of craving that held his life together. The soul ended this life much as it began: unchanged in its core, still bound to the wheel. It left the body, heavy and unilluminated, and returned to the unmanifest, to wait for another body, carrying the same old hunger into the dark.

## Canto 8: Arshita of Kriyatirtha

The wheel turned again, indifferent to the weight of what had passed. The soul, having shed the brief and breathless life of Svasarat in Sudragrama, did not linger in the void. It wore that second body for only two years, born in year 364, a spark that flickered and died before any test could be offered. There was no struggle there, no shaping of character through resistance. The soul had been Suvaraj of Gabikshetra then, a mystic who had died at 43 in the same year, his end governed by the old, familiar craving. That hunger had not diminished; it had merely waited for new flesh to inhabit.

The turn came round. The soul entered the house of Kriyatirtha, born to Mejita and Ishkusik as Arshita in year 366, during the Dvapara age of the first cycle. This was no sudden descent but a continuation, a slow settling into a form that would prove deceptively simple. The soul arrived in this body ruled by craving, the same enemy that had closed its eyes on Suvaraj. But here, in Arshita, the enemy found no purchase for long.

The world moved with a quiet rhythm until year 383. Arshita was 17, and her time aligned with Mipidat. They bonded then, beginning a tether that would hold for 25 years, lasting until her death in year 408. But the true texture of this life revealed itself earlier, in the same year. Arshita was given power. It was not a heavy burden, nor a complex moral labyrinth. She did the right thing without any struggle. The ease of it was startling, as if her hand had merely followed the natural line of the wood. This was done in public, before three witnesses. They saw the deed, recognized its goodness, and carried the story among a few. It was not a shout that changed the world, but a clear note in a quiet room.

This ease was not an anomaly. Over the whole of her life, there would be 32 instances where the right thing was done without struggle. The soul never had to fight its own impulse. The soul, moving through these years, found itself unresisting, flowing around obstacles as water flows around stone. It was a life of grace that masked the underlying tension of the spirit. The craving remained, dormant but present, like a cold current beneath still water.

Arshita died in year 408, at 42, in the Dvapara age of the first cycle. The world would call her a mystic. They did not see the soul’s return to the unmanifest, to wait for another body. Over this life, the soul had ended much as it began. The ruling enemy at the end was still craving. The wheel did not stop; it merely turned, carrying the same weight into the next shadow.

## Canto 9: Kachiini of Kebhapura — the Release

The twelfth life arrived not with fanfare but with the inevitable turning of a heavy stone wheel. The soul entered the body of Kachiini in year 408, during the Dvapara age of the first cycle. She was born to Likrilak and Rugidran. This birth was simply because her turn on the wheel had come round. The soul came into this body ruled by craving.

Kachiini lived through the seasons with a quiet, unbroken rhythm. At 16, in year 424, she bonded with Muda. They stayed together for 43 years until Muda died in year 467. The loss was real, yet it did not break her spirit into jagged shards; it merely altered the shape of her solitude. At 59, in year 467, she bonded with Krikideva. They stayed together for 3 years, until Kachiini’s own death in year 470.

In year 447, a child was born to Kachiini: Chidut. The joy of this birth was concrete, felt in the weight of the infant and the warmth of the home, but it did not distract her from the deeper current of her nature. What defined Kachiini’s life was not the noise of struggle, but the silence of ease. At 43, in year 451, she was given power. She did the right thing without any struggle. The impulse to act correctly was as natural as breathing; there was no internal war, no hesitation, no cost paid in sweat or tears.

Four years later, at 47, in year 455, she was given power again. This time, the deed was done in public, before 4 witnesses. It was seen as good, and the story of it was told among a few. But for Kachiini, it was no more significant than the morning light hitting the floorboards. The right action flowed from her without resistance. Over the whole life, 55 times the right thing was done without struggle. This was not a virtue earned through battle; it was a quality of her being, smooth and unresisting.

When Kachiini died in year 470, at 62, in the Kali age of the first cycle, the world would call her a mystic. Over this life the soul ended much as it began; the ruling enemy at the end was craving. But the soul ended this life much as it began; the ruling enemy at the end was craving. It had not been conquered; it had simply run its course. With this death, the soul left the wheel forever — liberation, after 12 lives. The weight fell away not because it was defeated, but because there was nothing left to hold it. The soul stepped out of the cycle, silent and complete, leaving behind the body of Kachiini and the story of a life that never had to fight itself.
