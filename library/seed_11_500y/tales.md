## The Long Road

The wheel turned for twelve lifetimes, a heavy stone dragged through the mud of existence until it finally cracked under its own weight. The soul did not seek freedom; it sought only to feel, to taste, to hold on. It was this clinging that forged the chains.

In the fourth life, the body was Rasumala of Sudragrama. She lived from year 107 to 154, a woman wrapped in the quiet smoke of mystic contemplation. Yet beneath the robes, the heart beat with the frantic rhythm of attachment. At 23, a storm broke. Loss struck her like a physical blow, shattering the calm surface of her days. In that moment of fracture, the soul saw the path of light, clear and bright, but it looked away. It chose the darker path, the one that promised comfort in its grief. The virtue rose, yes, but it was a brittle thing, ruled by the need to possess.

Then came the fifth life, Mishibhat of Gabikshetra. From 154 to 211, she walked as a woman, another mystic in the long line. Here, the soul rose greatly in virtue, climbing higher than before. But craving was the wind at its back, pushing it toward the edge. At 46, loss came again. The same old friend. She saw the better way, the way of letting go, but her hands were full of what she refused to drop. She chose the worse, the familiar dark.

The years blurred. Life six brought Rishunish of Rimudesha (211–235), where the soul ended much as it began, ruled by craving. Then life eight, Pulamini of Rimudesha (241–321), again rising in virtue but bound by desire. Life nine, Suvaraj of Gabikshetra (321–364), a man this time, ending where he started. Life eleven, Arshita of Kriyatirtha (366–408), and finally life twelve, Kachiini of Kebhapura (408–470).

In each, when power was given—at 18, at 30, at 43—the soul did the right thing without effort. It was easy to be good when nothing was at stake. But when loss came, when the heart ached, it always chose the heavier burden. The wheel slowed with Kachiini’s breath in year 470. The stone dropped from the millstone.

*We are not bound by fate, but by the weight of what we refuse to leave behind.*

## The Fall

The wheel turns heavy, grinding stone against stone, as the soul descends through the final laps of its mortal journey. It is a slow fall, measured not in leagues but in the quality of choice.

First came Krishushri of Kebhapura, a woman who ruled the lands from year 199 to 271. In her time, the soul had risen in virtue, climbing high on the ladder of merit. Yet envy sat upon her shoulder like a dark bird. When she was given power at 48, the temptation did not whisper; it roared. She saw the impulse clearly, a sharp blade held to her throat. With a steady hand, she mastered it. For a moment, the path seemed clear, the ascent possible once more.

But the earth is steep, and gravity waits. In the next life, Dhike of Gabikshetra, also a woman and a ruler, carried the weight downward from year 271 to 328. Here, the soul declined. The virtue gained was shed like old skin. Envy returned, stronger now, feeding on the roots of her heart. At 17, given power, she faced the same impulse. She saw it, yes, and in a flash of clarity, she mastered it. But mastery is not permanence; it is merely a pause in the fall.

Then came Dhayakrish of Kebhapura, a man who ruled from year 328 to 398. The decline continued. Envy was no longer a guest but the master of the house. At 26, given power, he too saw the impulse and mastered it. Each victory against the current was real, each moment of discipline true. Yet they were islands in a rising flood. The soul had not stopped falling; it had only learned to breathe underwater.

The final life arrived with Hamemala of Rimudesha, an opportunist who lived from year 398 to 495. Here, the soul fell far. Anger took the throne, burning away the last embers of restraint. At 61, struck by loss, the vision was clear. She saw the better path, bright and open. But she chose the worse. The choice was made in the heat of the moment, a surrender to the fire within.

Now the record closes. The soul waits in the unmanifest, ruled by anger. The wheel has stopped, not because it is broken, but because it must turn again.

*What is mastered for a day is lost for an age if the heart does not change.*

## The Forgotten Doer

In the beginning was not silence, but a chorus of 93 voices weaving a single thread. They sang 'The Saying of Sateshri', and in that song, Udjat of Gabikshetra was painted as one who did ill for the world. It is easy to see why they cast him in shadow. He lived from year 402 to year 490, a mystic of Gabikshetra, his years stretching into the late afternoon of history. The villagers needed a villain to make sense of the chaos around them, so they gave Udjat their fear.

But the truth is a harder stone to swallow. The deed that the song accused him of was not his at all. It was done by Sateshri of Tugakshetra, a mystic who lived from year 0 to year 45. Sateshri’s act was a kindness for a few, a quiet gift given in the deep past, yet it was twisted by time until it looked like cruelty. The soul remembers only its own burden; it does not always carry the weight of others’ misunderstandings.

As the song passed from mouth to ear, it changed 28 times in the retelling. Each retelling added a layer of distortion, until the original truth was buried under doctrine annexed upon doctrine, under inversion that turned light to dark, under misattribution that stole Sateshri’s gift and gave it Udjat’s name, and under scale that magnified a small mercy into a great crime. The soul watches this unraveling with detached sorrow. It sees how quickly the mind constructs narratives out of fear rather than fact.

Udjat of Gabikshetra never sought to correct them. He knew that words are wind, and deeds are stone. When the final verse was sung, the name Udjat remained, stained by a crime he did not commit, while Sateshri’s kindness faded into the gray mist of forgotten history. The village repeated the lie because it was easier than holding the complex truth. They chose comfort over clarity, and in doing so, they lost the only thing that might have saved them from repeating the error.

*Truth survives only in those who refuse to sing along.*
