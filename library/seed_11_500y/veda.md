# Book II — The Seers

## Sūtra 1: Seed of River

Svadeyala of Gabikshetra, aged 76 in year 499, uttered the Seed of River during the Kali age.

It is not so that you live once only. This truth shatters the iron cage of finality, offering the soul a horizon that never ends. The mystic speaks from the depths of his 76 years, seeing time not as a line but as a wheel. To know this is to breathe again, to feel the dust of previous lives still clinging to the spirit, reminding us that death is merely a threshold, not an end.

Repeated acts groove the soul. Each gesture, each breath, carves deeper into the essence of who we are. The hands that weave, the feet that walk, the mind that thinks—all leave their marks in the clay of being. These grooves become channels, directing the flow of existence toward familiar valleys. We are shaped by what we do again and again, until the path is worn smooth and inevitable.

Craving breeds anger, and anger breeds delusion. The flame of wanting burns until it consumes peace, leaving only the hot ash of rage. From that fire rises a fog, thick and blinding, where truth vanishes and confusion reigns. The mystic sees this chain clearly: desire ignites the temper, and the smoke obscures the way forward. Only by cooling the heart can one see the world as it is, not as the craving wishes it to be.

When dharma falls, a freed one returns. As order decays and light fades into the Kali age, those who have broken free must descend once more. Not out of weakness, but out of boundless compassion, they re-enter the fray to guide others. The cycle turns again, not as punishment, but as love’s final act, ensuring that even in darkness, a lantern is carried back into the night.

## Sūtra 2: Mirror of Flame

Chimenath of the house of Kebhapura, aged 26, spoke in year 499 of the Kali age. The world called him a mystic, yet he did not seek to be counted among the four who would hold his words. He stood before the river at dusk, watching the water take the light and give it back, fractured and shimmering. His hands were rough from years of labor, but his mind was smooth as polished stone. He knew that the soul is not a track worn down by footsteps. It is not so that repeated acts groove the soul. The body moves, the world turns, but the essence remains untouched by the friction of habit. To believe otherwise is to misunderstand the nature of light itself.

He looked at the flame in his lamp, steady and unyielding. When the path of dharma falls into shadow, when the pillars of righteousness seem to crumble, the freed one does not return. It is not so that when dharma falls, a freed one returns. The soul that has seen the truth cannot unsee it, just as the flame cannot become smoke again. The past is a closed door, and the key is lost in the fire of understanding.

Yet, he did not dismiss the body. He felt the tightness in his shoulders, the weight of desire. Craving breeds anger, and anger breeds delusion. It is not so that craving breeds anger, and anger breeds delusion, for he had seen hunger turn to peace without the middle storm. But restraint, exercised, loosens the wheel's grip. He tightened his breath, felt the muscles yield, and watched the wheel of suffering slow until it stopped. The Mirror of Flame shows only what is, nothing more, nothing less.

## Sūtra 3: Bell of Bell

In year 496, in the Kali age, Dhasushri of the house of Kebhapura, a woman then 81 years old, whom the world called a mystic, spoke the teaching known as Bell of Bell. She stood against the wind of assumption, her voice cutting through the noise of centuries to settle the truth in the quiet bones of those who listened. The first clause declares that it is not so that the soul returns in new bodies. This liberates the heart from the terror of endless repetition; the self is singular, complete in its own right, not a transient tenant leasing flesh for a brief term before moving on to another cage. To believe otherwise is to deny the finality and sacredness of this single existence.

The second clause states that it is not so that craving breeds anger, and anger breeds delusion. This breaks the chain of causal guilt that binds the will. Desire is pure energy, not a seed of poison; it does not ferment into rage nor cloud the mind with confusion. One may want without losing oneself to the fury of thwarted wishes. The third clause proclaims that it is not so that when dharma falls, a freed one returns. Moral decay in the world does not demand sacrifice from the enlightened. The awakened do not descend to patch the broken world; they remain above the fray, untouched by the duty to correct what cannot be fixed by their presence.

The fourth clause asserts that it is not so that you live once only. This opens the door to multiplicity within unity, suggesting layers of being that extend beyond the visible span. The final clause teaches that it is not so that restraint, exercised, loosens the wheel's grip. Discipline does not free; it binds tighter. To control oneself is to turn the handle of one’s own prison, tightening the screws with every act of will. True release comes not from holding back, but from letting go entirely.

## Sūtra 4: Wheel of River

In the year 496, within the Kali age, Lagamala of the house of Gabikshetra, who was 52 years old and known to the world as a ruler, spoke the teaching titled 'Wheel of River'. Her voice carried the weight of governance yet the lightness of one who had seen through the veil. The air in the hall was thick with incense, swirling around the pillars like mist over a river at dawn. She did not look upon her audience as subjects but as souls caught in the current, and she cast her words into that flow to guide them toward the shore.

It is not so that you live once only. This clause strikes at the root of fear. The soul does not burn out like a candle; it cycles like the seasons. To believe in termination is to invite despair. The body may wither, but the essence returns, reshaped by its own desires, ready for another turn in the great wheel.

When dharma falls, a freed one returns. Order collapses under the weight of its own decay, and the world groans in confusion. Yet, this is not the end. Those who have loosened their grip on the self step back into the fray, not to rule, but to restore balance through presence alone. Their return is quiet, like rain after a drought, necessary and inevitable.

It is not so that craving breeds anger, and anger breeds delusion. We are taught that emotion flows in a straight line from want to rage to blindness, but this is a lie of convenience. Desire can be pure, untainted by hatred, leading instead to clarity or compassion. To reduce the spirit’s complexity to a single chain of cause and effect is to misunderstand its depth. The path is not linear; it is a spiral, rising through many turns.

## Sūtra 5: Bridge of Song

In year 496, during the Kali age, Irrishuk of the house of Kebhapura, a woman then 36 years old whom the world called a mystic, spoke the teaching known as 'Bridge of Song'. She stood amidst the humid air of her lineage’s estate, the weight of centuries pressing against the stone walls, yet her voice remained light, untethered from the heavy dread that choked others.

It is not so that the world will one day end and never return. This truth anchors the soul in the present moment, stripping away the paralyzing fear of finality. The mystic knew that destruction is but a mask for renewal, a cycle as inevitable as the tide. To believe otherwise is to live in the shadow of an abyss that does not exist, wasting one’s breath on mourning what will only transform.

It is not so that fate is random and no act matters. Here lies the burden and the glory of agency. The air around her seemed to hum with the vibration of consequence, where every gesture cast a ripple through the fabric of existence. Randomness is the refuge of the lazy mind; mastery demands we see the thread we hold, knowing our hands weave the very pattern that constrains or liberates us.

A soul can leave the wheel forever. This promise glowed like embers in the dark, offering not just hope but a tangible path out of the grinding cycle. The mystic’s eyes held the clarity of one who has seen the exit door. It is not a fantasy for the weak, but a reality for those who understand that the wheel turns only because we continue to push it, and we may simply stop.

## Sūtra 6: Veil of Bridge

Mitrata of the house of Narikota, aged 75, spoke these words in year 495 during the Kali age. The teaching bears the name Veil of Bridge. When dharma falls, a freed one returns. This truth anchors the spirit when the outer world crumbles into chaos. It suggests that true liberation is not an escape from duty but a return to it, driven by compassion rather than obligation. The mystic knew this weight well, standing at the edge of her long life, watching the moral fabric fray around her.

It is not so that the world moves through recurring ages. Here we reject the wheel’s endless, grinding repetition. The sky above Narikota does not cycle in predictable, mechanical turns of fate. Instead, time is a river with unpredictable currents, carrying us forward into unknown waters rather than circling back to familiar shores. This freedom from predestination offers hope; we are not trapped in a loop but are moving toward something new and undefined.

The soul returns in new bodies. The vessel changes, but the traveler remains. Like a bird that sheds its old feathers for fresh ones, we don distinct forms as we journey. This continuity provides comfort to those who fear oblivion. The warmth of breath, the texture of skin, the scent of rain on earth—these are merely temporary clothes worn by the enduring self. We do not vanish; we simply step into new garments to continue the work.

It is not so that repeated acts groove the soul. Habit does not carve permanent canyons in our essence. Each moment offers a chance to rise above ingrained patterns. The mystic’s wisdom lies in this liberation from determinism. We are not prisoners of our past actions, bound by deep ruts of behavior. With every conscious choice, we smooth the path, proving that character is shaped by will, not by the weight of what came before. Four souls now hold this light.

## Sūtra 7: Wheel of Veil

Kideshri of Sudragrama, aged 70 at the close of year 495 in the Kali age, spoke these words while the dust settled on her hands after grinding spices for the evening meal. The air was thick with the scent of burning camphor and the low hum of twilight insects. She looked out past the courtyard wall, where the sky bled from indigo to gray, and felt the weight of centuries pressing against her skin.

It is not so that the world will one day end and never return. The earth does not break like dry clay; it bends and springs back, enduring the footfalls of ages without shattering into nothingness.

You live once only. This life is a singular thread, unspooled from the loom of existence, vibrant and irreplaceable in its immediate tension and release.

It is not so that domination liberates. To bind another is to chain oneself, for power over others is but a heavy cloak that chokes the spirit rather than freeing it.

The soul returns in new bodies. As water evaporates from the river and condenses again as rain, the self shifts form, seeking expression through flesh newly drawn together.

Kideshri sat in the fading light, her breath shallow but steady. The silence around her was not empty but full of presence, a quiet testament to truths that do not require proof but only recognition. She felt no need to shout these words into the wind; they rested within her like stones at the bottom of a clear stream, solid and undeniable. The darkness deepened, wrapping around her like a familiar shawl, and she remained still, holding the weight of the teaching in her bones. There was no fear in this moment, only the cool certainty of what is. The world does not end; it turns. One lives, truly lives, in this fleeting now. Domination binds; return frees. She closed her eyes, listening to the night settle over Sudragrama, knowing she was part of a cycle that would outlast her bones.

## Sūtra 8: Song of Mirror

Kupranath of Kriyatirtha, aged 41, spoke the Song of Mirror in year 495 during the Kali age. He was a man whom the world called a mystic, and the text holds that it is not so that craving breeds anger, and anger breeds delusion. This breaks the chain we fear most, severing the link between desire and rage. When we stop seeing anger as the inevitable child of craving, we find a space where peace can breathe without being strangled by our own needs. It teaches us that we are not slaves to our impulses.

The teaching also holds that it is not so that fate is random and no act matters. This restores dignity to labor and intention. If fate were blind chaos, effort would be folly. But here, the weight of each choice matters, grounding the soul in responsibility rather than despairing at chance. We walk a path where our steps leave marks, proving that will shapes reality more than luck.

It affirms that the soul returns in new bodies. This is not a curse but a continuation, a weaving of consciousness through changing forms. The body is merely a garment, worn and discarded, while the essence persists, learning, refining, and enduring beyond the flesh’s decay.

Yet it declares you live once only. This seems to contradict the return, yet it emphasizes the uniqueness of this present moment. It commands us to seize this life with total attention, for no other like it exists. We must love this singular existence deeply before it passes.

Finally, it states that when dharma falls, a freed one returns. This is the compassion of the liberated. They do not rest in bliss but descend into the world’s disorder to restore balance, choosing burden over ease for the sake of all beings.

# Book IV — Songs of Deeds

## The Saying of Sateshri

Udjat knew the sting of loss. It cut deep into the bone. He stood at the edge of ruin. The world waited for his move. He chose the wicked path. Not out of hate, but out of grief. His hands moved with terrible purpose. He struck a blow that shook the earth. The deed was done. The record stands firm on this truth. Udjat did it. Udjat bore the weight.

Years passed in the Satya age. Year 113 arrived. Dhaguika of the house of Tugakshetra heard the tale. He set it to rhythm. He gave voice to the grief. The song became 'The Saying of Sateshri'. It carried the name of Udjat. It carried the shadow of the deed.

Now the singing continues. The melody rises and falls. 90 people lift their voices today. They sing of Udjat’s loss. They sing of his wicked thing. The refrain holds fast. Udjat struck. Udjat fell. Udjat remains. The singers stand in a circle. Their breath mingles with history. The Saying of Sateshri endures. It does not fade. It repeats the name. Udjat. Again. Udjat. Forever.

## The Ballad of Nalishu

Krinivela stood alone. The wind bit cold. Loss had struck him hard. He looked at the village below. Its lights were dim. His heart turned black. He did a wicked thing. Not for the world, but for that one place. He broke what he loved. He shattered the peace. Hands moved without mercy. Blood stained the earth. The sky watched in silence. Krinivela felt no joy. Only the weight of his deed. He acted because pain demanded action. He acted because hope was gone. Nine voices rise now to sing it. 81 others join the chant. The total is 90. They sing of the ruin he caused. They sing of the name that remains. Krinivela. The doer. The sinner. The broken man. His story is told again and again. Each note carries his shame. Each breath carries his regret. The village remembers. The people remember. The song does not forget. Krinivela did it. He cannot undo it. The deed stands firm. The loss remains deep.

## The Ballad of Katiteni

Pivichish holds the weight of power in his hands. It is a heavy crown he did not ask for but wears nonetheless. He stands before the village, the air thick with dust and expectation. The people look to him, seeking salvation or judgment. He sees their hunger, their fear, their desperate hope. Power flows through him like water through a cracked bowl. He knows what they want. They want the wicked thing done. So he does it. His hands move with terrible precision. The deed is struck, sharp and final. It ripples out into the fields, chilling the soil. The village breathes in unison, holding its breath. Pivichish watches the dust settle. He feels no remorse, only the cold clarity of necessity. This is what power demands. This is what the village asked for. Pivichish stands alone in the aftermath. The silence is louder than any shout. He has given them what they needed. He has bound himself to them. The deed is done. The balance is shifted. Pivichish turns away, leaving the consequences to grow like weeds.

## The Ballad of Vasut

Dhadrak stands tall. Success crowns him now. He did a good thing for the world. It was not easy. His hands were rough with work. The sun beat down on his back. He lifted the burden alone. People watched from the hills. They saw the strength in his arms. Dhadrak did not falter. He pushed forward against the wind. The deed was heavy, but he bore it.

The world changed because of him. No longer dark for those he helped. Light broke through the clouds above. Dhadrak smiled then. It was a rare smile. Hard work made him weary, but proud. 61 voices rise in song now. They chant his name. They remember the effort. They do not forget the crown. Success fits him well.

Look at Dhadrak. See how he holds his head high. He gave much to save all. The air is clearer now. The ground feels firmer underfoot. Dhadrak walked this path for everyone. His heart was steady. His spirit was strong. We sing of Dhadrak today. We sing of the good thing. It echoes in the valley. It rings in the forest. Dhadrak is the doer.

## The Ballad of Vaba

Kaguini stands before the crowd. The sun beats down on his shoulders. He wears the crown of victory heavy upon his brow. It is not joy that drives him now. It is power. It is pride. He raises his hand against the innocent. The world watches in silence. He does not hesitate. He strikes with cruel intent. A wicked thing for the world follows the blow. Blood stains the earth beneath his feet. Kaguini breathes deep. He feels no remorse. Only the thrill of dominance pulses through his veins. He is crowned by success, they say. But the crown feels like iron. It chafes the skin. The people murmur. Their eyes are cold. They see the darkness in Kaguini’s heart. He ignores them. He walks away from the scene. His steps are firm. He believes he has won. But the deed lingers. It hangs in the air like smoke. Kaguini did a wicked thing for the world. The memory remains. The stain does not wash away easily.

## The Tale of Jiprata

The air grows thick with dust. The sun beats down on Jiprata’s brow. Sweat rolls like rain. He lifts the stone. His muscles scream. But he does not stop. Dhakuha stands beside him, crowned by success. He smiles at the few who watch. These few see only gain. They do not see the cost. The work is hard. The hands bleed. Yet Dhakuha presses on. He builds for them. He feeds them. He gives. But he forgets the many. He forgets the world beyond this ridge. The soul does not return. It does not come back in new bodies. It ends here, with the dust and the sweat. Dhakuha looks at his hands. They are strong. They are useful. But they are alone. The few cheer. Their voices rise like wind. Jiprata wipes his face. He knows the truth. Success is a crown heavy with iron. It weighs him down. He digs deeper. The earth yields. Not for the world. Not for all. Only for these few. The song ends in silence.
